What it is

Built to fit the plant you already have

Sentys works with the sensor data your control system is already logging, needs no failure history to learn from, and replaces nothing you run today.

Learns healthy · flags drift

A baseline per machine, from its own history

It learns what healthy looks like from your machine's own history, including its different running modes (winter load, summer load), so a change of mode is not mistaken for a fault. Two identical pumps are not identical to Sentys: each gets its own healthy. It doesn't need failure labels; most sites don't have any.

Instead of an anomaly score, the output reads like triage: “Vibration: 3.2× healthy baseline; temperature and flow within range.”

Sensor-agnostic

Any tags your system logs

Temperatures, pressures, flow, current or vibration, exported from your control system (SCADA or SRO), its history database, or a plain spreadsheet. A new sensor type is a settings change, not new software.

European exports parse natively: semicolons, decimal commas, your column names in your language. CSV or JSON, straight from the system you have.

Why this matters

Alarms watch one gauge at a time

The alarms in your control room are lines drawn on single gauges: fire when a temperature passes a set point, when a pressure drops below one. Drawn loose, the line is crossed late, when the damage is done. Drawn tight, it fires so often that people stop looking. Neither sees the way machines actually fail: slowly, on several gauges at once, each still inside its line.

Servicing on a calendar doesn't close the gap either. It replaces healthy parts and still gets surprised by the one machine that fails early. Sentys learns what all of a machine's gauges look like together when it is healthy, and reports how far they have drifted from that, gauge by gauge. It sits beside your alarms and replaces none of them.

Checked against the product pages of the Danish SRO integrators (Coseno, DME) and IGSS: condition monitoring there means threshold and template alarms on single tags. None of them learns what normal looks like for a machine.

Evidence, translated

Tested on six public datasets, published in full

Most predictive-maintenance tools need a catalogue of past failures to learn from, the one thing a well-run plant rarely has. Sentys learns the shape of healthy operation instead. Whether that works is a question for held-out data, so here is all of it:

Six public datasets covering four machine classes, run through one unmodified pipeline, trained on healthy data only every time. Outcomes in plain terms first; the technical numbers sit in the small print for your advisor.

30/30flagged before failure

Turbofan engines · NASA C-MAPSS

Every one of 30 engines the model had never seen was flagged before it failed, with a median of 59 operating cycles of warning. With alarm thresholds set from healthy data alone, one engine in the fleet false-alarmed.

For your advisor: on engines it had never seen, it ranks a failing engine above a healthy one 99.6% of the time (AUC 0.996), and its ranking tracks remaining life at 0.762 out of 1.0 (Spearman ρ). On the harder set with six operating conditions (FD002): AUC 0.95, ρ 0.615, with no tuning for the conditions.
75 hof warning on the first rig

Bearing vibration · NASA IMS

On run-to-failure rigs, the bearing that actually failed was ranked strongest-trending and loudest, flagged 75 hours before end of run on one rig and ~470 on the other. The spectral sense fired first, reproducing known bearing-defect physics with zero labels.

Limits, stated: bearings on the same shaft also rise late in the run (the ranked view still picks the right one by 2–3×), and a healthy baseline learned on one installation doesn't transfer to another. Each machine gets its own, which is the deployment protocol anyway.
±0.03of the specialist audio tool's score (AUC)

Industrial pump audio · MIMII / DCASE

On four physically different pumps in factory noise, the same pipeline, with zero audio-specific engineering, landed within 0.03 average AUC of the purpose-built DCASE audio baseline, beating it outright on two of the four.

For your advisor: average AUC 0.687 against 0.715 for the purpose-built baseline (AUC = how often a faulty pump is ranked above a healthy one; 1.0 is perfect). Also measured: a brand-new pump gets useful day-one coverage from its fleet-mates' shared model (AUC 0.60 unseen); its own baseline lifts that to 0.69. Cold-start from the fleet, precision from the fine-tune.
The other three: a CNC pass and the two bearing tests we failed, with the numbers
PHM 2010 · CNC tool wearGATE PASS

Raw 50 kHz data from a physical CNC rig: the same pipeline tracked a measured tool-wear curve on 3 unseen cutters and alarmed on all three with zero false alarms, at 46–62% of end-of-life wear.

For your advisor: the signal tracked measured wear at 0.972 / 0.975 / 0.955 out of 1.0 (Spearman ρ) · AUC 1.000 · pass marks set before the run.

Detection worked: 10 of 11 unseen bearings flagged, one false alarm in healthy windows, and the rig's operating conditions were found without being told. One of our own pass marks, set before the run, was missed (a pooled score of AUC 0.665 against a higher bar we had set), so the run is reported as a fail, with the number.

XJTU-SY · bearing run-to-failureBAR MISSED — PUBLISHED

Detection worked here too: 9 of 9 unseen bearings flagged, one false alarm, with no labels of any kind. The same pooled score missed our bar (AUC 0.659). Published as measured.

We kill our own metrics before a skeptic can. Every benchmark ran against a pre-registered gate. Two of six missed a bar; both are published with the number that missed, and your data would be held to the same standard.

“How much warning do I actually get?”

On the turbofan benchmark, the median first warning came 59 cycles before failure, roughly the last fifth of the machine's life; on the bearing rigs, days to weeks. What Sentys does not do yet is date the failure. It ranks which machine is drifting and shows which sensor saw it first, so you inspect the right asset while there is still time to schedule the work. “Fails in 12 days” is a claim we can't prove yet, so we don't make it. The number we watch hardest is false alarms: a tool that cries wolf gets switched off, and then it catches nothing.

Claude drafts · Code decides

The AI that can't make numbers up

The prose in your report is written by Claude; the numbers never are. Every figure is computed by the pipeline and checked against the run's own evidence before the report goes out. And no action is taken on a machine without your engineer signing off.

How your data is handled

  • Processed on a single machine in Denmark. Your raw data is never uploaded to any public cloud; if you prefer a hosted setup, a dedicated European server (Hetzner) is available on request.
  • Read-only, no control path. Sentys reads sensor data and never touches your controls. For a utility under NIS2, that keeps it out of the critical path.
  • Stated up front: to work out what each column is, Sentys sends column names, per-column statistics and a handful of sample values to an AI service, never your bulk data. If even that is too much, say so and it's done by hand.
  • Used for your analysis only. Never used to train models for anyone else. Deleted when you say so, confirmed in writing.
